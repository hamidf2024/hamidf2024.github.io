Project 13 Research: Building a New Model

A dedicated phase for one job: develop new models that pick day trades in stocks running hard at the open, and test them so honestly that a good result can be trusted. Five cycles so far, all rejected. Together they show that long trades in stocks running at the open have no edge in any design or with any input tried.

How the research works

Two separate paths, the same five steps

StepPath A: original design from scratchPath B: combine strengths of existing models
1. Define successAt least +0.5% per trade after 0.5% cost, profitable in 5 of 6 development years and on unseen data; then the major goal
2. BaselineRule strategy S8, gradient boosting, a small transformer, a GRUThe same, plus each reviewed model run on our data
3. HypothesisNew mechanisms from problems we saw: cross-stock attention, regime gate, fade-state inputs, cost-aware outputCombinations: patch tokens + market-context gating, tree features as tokens, pretrained forecasts
4. Test one changeNew mechanism vs the standard transformerEach borrowed part on and off; vs its sources and a simple blend
5. Refine and scaleKeep what passes, drop what adds nothing, grow only after a gate review

Unseen data

Jan 2025 – Nov 2026 is never used to build or choose a model. Development uses 2016–2024; each finished model gets one week-by-week run on the unseen period.

Fair tests

A plan written before each run, one change at a time, a leakage checklist, at least 3 random seeds, costs of 0.25%, 0.5% and 1%, and every result kept, including failures.

Decision gates

G0 setup → G1 plan → G2 beats baselines on 2019–2024 → G3 holds on unseen data → G4 forward test. A model must pass each in order.

Cycle 1 result: model A1

Verdict

A1, a transformer that compares every stock running the same morning, was rejected. It beat the same model without that layer in only 3 of 6 years, and no model made money in any year.

The trade and the test

Long at the 10:00 open, +5% target, −3% stop, out by 10:30, on stocks that ran 10%+ in the first 15 minutes. The design was chosen on 2016–2018 only. Each model was trained on earlier years and tested on 2019–2024, one year at a time.

Why it failed

What the diagnosis found

  1. The models learn how much a stock will move, not which way. Ranked by expected profit they pick quiet stocks that lose a little; ranked by chance of hitting the target they pick volatile ones that also hit the stop more.
  2. The cross-stock layer had nothing to work with. A1's rankings within a morning matched the base model's (correlation 0.93). Both found the morning's best stock less often than random.
  3. The money is there, the inputs can't see it. A perfect top-10% pick makes +$39.60 per trade; the models' picks gained only about 0.1% before a 1% round-trip cost.
  4. The trade-count rule was unstable (17 to 1,180 trades a year), so the next test picks the top stock each morning.

What it changes

A smarter architecture cannot create direction information that isn't in the inputs. The next version therefore changes the information, not the model: each stock's multi-day history.

One condition did hold up: stocks that had already faded at least 10% from their high lost much less (−$4.5 vs −$10.1 per trade), so it stays part of the design.

Cycle 2 result: model A5

Verdict

A5 added each stock's multi-day history (20 features from the previous 60 trading days) to both the transformer and gradient boosting. Neither improved, so A5 was rejected.

Why

Tested one at a time, every multi-day feature mainly predicts how big the move will be, not which way: features pointing to bigger moves point to worse results on a +5% / −3% trade, because bigger moves hit the stop more. The models used the new inputs heavily but only learned volatility more precisely.

What two cycles show: price and volume history, from the first 30 minutes and the previous 60 days, does not reveal which way these stocks go after 10:00. The next input has to be a different kind of information: news and catalysts, float and short interest, or the short side, where the data already shows a reliable fade.

Cycle 3 result: model A6

Verdict

A6 added news and catalysts (67,122 headlines published before 10:00) to both models. Both were better in 4 of 6 years, but by amounts indistinguishable from zero, so A6 was rejected.

Why

The kind of news barely separates winners from losers. Where it seems to help (earnings, analyst news), those stocks simply move less, so they lose less on a +5% / −3% trade, the same quiet-stock effect as before. Win rates did not rise.

What three cycles show: the first 30 minutes, 60 days of history, and the news all fail to reveal which way these stocks go between 10:00 and 10:30. The reliable pattern is the fade. The next step changes the trade itself: buying after the fade ends, holding longer, and bigger targets, which the 20%-a-week goal needs anyway. That requires full-day price data.

Cycle 4 result: 480 long designs on full-day data

Verdict

With the 10:30 exit now flexible, 480 long designs were tested on 2016–2018: buying after the fade, on a new high, at fixed times, holding to the close, targets up to +20%. None made money in any year at 0.5% cost, so no model was built on them.

Why

These stocks keep drifting down for the rest of the day. Every entry has a negative average move before costs, so no target, stop or holding time can make a long trade profitable on average. Buying after the fade does not mark the bottom, and holding longer only adds to the loss.

What four cycles show: for stocks that run hard at the open, long trades have no edge in any design or with any input tried. The same downward drift is why the short side works. A long-only path needs a different set of stocks.

Cycle 5 result: a new architecture, A7

Verdict

A7, a Direction-Disentangled Transformer, splits what it learns into a "direction" channel and a "move size" channel, with an adversary that keeps move size out of the direction channel. It was rejected: −$10.41 per trade vs −$9.60 for the base model, better in only 2 of 6 years.

Why, and what it taught

The architecture trained as designed, but its direction score stayed tied to move size. The reason is the target: on a +5% / −3% trade, "did the trade make money" depends on how much a stock moves, so the label itself rewards volatility and no adversary can remove that.

Lesson: to learn direction, the model needs a direction-only target, such as the return divided by the stock's own volatility. That is the next version (A7-v2), to be applied to the new stock sets as their data arrives.
