Project 13 Research: Building a New Model

The goal is a model that makes money on long day trades in stocks that run hard at the open, tested so honestly that a good result can be trusted. Every model is listed below with its result, why it worked or failed, and what it taught the next one.

Where we stand

The goal, step by step

Net return per trade after 0.5% cost per side, one trade a day, the whole account each trade. The major goal (20% a week) needs about +3.7% per trade.

MilestoneNet per tradeStatus

In plain words

What these stocks do: stocks that jump 10% or more in the first 15 minutes tend to drift down for the rest of the day. Buying them at a fixed time loses money in every year tested.

What works so far: choosing when to buy during the day. Timing models find moments that do rise, enough to cover costs on paper.

What is still missing: a model whose profit also holds on data it has never seen. The best one (A10) passed on 2019–2024 but failed on 2025–2026, because part of its edge came from thinly traded moments that cannot really be bought.

Current loop: until the first milestone

Since Nov 7, 2026 the research runs as a loop: learn from every model and from outside research, design a new model, write its plan before running it, test it on 2019–2024 at real fills, record why it worked or not, and repeat. A model reaching M1 gets one test on unseen data; only a pass there counts. The view of the problem changes when progress stalls.

A8 changed the question to when to buy: −$1.79 per trade (real fills). A9 added "when to sell" but ranked on volatility-scaled gains: −$9.50. Details in earlier cycles.

What we have learned

  1. These stocks fade.After a 10%+ opening run they drift down for the rest of the day, so long trades at a fixed time start behind.
  2. Models learn size, not direction.Price history, multi-day context and news mostly tell how much a stock will move, not which way.
  3. Ask "when", not only "which".Choosing the moment to buy (A8) cut the loss per trade by about 80%.
  4. Use direction-only targets.Predict the return divided by the stock's own volatility; never rank trades on anything multiplied by volatility (A9).
  5. The decision rule matters as much as the model.Fixing only A9's buy rule moved it from −$9.50 to −$0.34 per trade.
  6. A second opinion helps.A judge trained on past proposals picks better trades (A10), but only if what it learns from is real.
  7. Only real prices count.Thinly traded moments show lagging prices that look like predictable rises; labels and tests must use executable prices (A10 unseen test).
  8. Research agrees, and points at costs.Published intraday strategies that work (e.g. opening-range breakouts on "stocks in play") trade liquid stocks at commission-level costs; replications that charge spreads find the edge disappears. Full review: research/LITERATURE_REVIEW.md.
  9. Big moves are predictable, their order is not.Half of the stocks up 20%+ by 09:45 rise another 10% later (two-thirds in the likeliest group), but whether a sharp drop comes first cannot be told from their characteristics.
  10. Risk management shapes results, it does not create an edge.On 20%+ runners, 58 stop, target, trailing and scale-out designs all lose after costs; half of the eventual winners first fall 5%.
  11. Time and gain show room to move, not direction.Earlier and bigger gainers are far more likely to rise another 10% (up to two-thirds), but just as likely to drop first.
  12. Costs decide everything.At 0.5% per side a trade must gain about 1% just to break even; at 0.25% several models would already be profitable.

Full record of every model's pros, cons and lesson: research/LESSONS_LEARNED.md in the project repository.

How we test

Two separate paths, the same five steps

StepPath A: original design from scratchPath B: combine strengths of existing models
1. Define successAt least +0.5% per trade after 0.5% cost, profitable in 5 of 6 development years and on unseen data; then the major goal
2. BaselineRule strategy S8, gradient boosting, a small transformer, a GRUThe same, plus each reviewed model run on our data
3. HypothesisNew mechanisms from problems we saw: cross-stock attention, regime gate, fade-state inputs, cost-aware outputCombinations: patch tokens + market-context gating, tree features as tokens, pretrained forecasts
4. Test one changeNew mechanism vs the standard transformerEach borrowed part on and off; vs its sources and a simple blend
5. Refine and scaleKeep what passes, drop what adds nothing, grow only after a gate review

Unseen data

Jan 2025 – Nov 2026 is never used to build or choose a model. Development uses 2016–2024; each finished model gets one week-by-week run on the unseen period.

Fair tests

A plan written before each run, one change at a time, a leakage checklist, at least 3 random seeds, costs of 0.25%, 0.5% and 1%, and every result kept, including failures.

Decision gates

G0 setup → G1 plan → G2 beats baselines on 2019–2024 → G3 holds on unseen data → G4 forward test. A model must pass each in order.

Stage 1: fixed-time models (cycles 1–5)

Cycle 1 result: model A1

Verdict

A1, a transformer that compares every stock running the same morning, was rejected. It beat the same model without that layer in only 3 of 6 years, and no model made money in any year.

The trade and the test

Long at the 10:00 open, +5% target, −3% stop, out by 10:30, on stocks that ran 10%+ in the first 15 minutes. The design was chosen on 2016–2018 only. Each model was trained on earlier years and tested on 2019–2024, one year at a time.

Why it failed

What the diagnosis found

  1. The models learn how much a stock will move, not which way. Ranked by expected profit they pick quiet stocks that lose a little; ranked by chance of hitting the target they pick volatile ones that also hit the stop more.
  2. The cross-stock layer had nothing to work with. A1's rankings within a morning matched the base model's (correlation 0.93). Both found the morning's best stock less often than random.
  3. The money is there, the inputs can't see it. A perfect top-10% pick makes +$39.60 per trade; the models' picks gained only about 0.1% before a 1% round-trip cost.
  4. The trade-count rule was unstable (17 to 1,180 trades a year), so the next test picks the top stock each morning.

What it changes

A smarter architecture cannot create direction information that isn't in the inputs. The next version therefore changes the information, not the model: each stock's multi-day history.

One condition did hold up: stocks that had already faded at least 10% from their high lost much less (−$4.5 vs −$10.1 per trade), so it stays part of the design.

Why

Tested one at a time, every multi-day feature mainly predicts how big the move will be, not which way: features pointing to bigger moves point to worse results on a +5% / −3% trade, because bigger moves hit the stop more. The models used the new inputs heavily but only learned volatility more precisely.

What two cycles show: price and volume history, from the first 30 minutes and the previous 60 days, does not reveal which way these stocks go after 10:00. The next input has to be a different kind of information: news and catalysts, float and short interest, or the short side, where the data already shows a reliable fade.

Why

The kind of news barely separates winners from losers. Where it seems to help (earnings, analyst news), those stocks simply move less, so they lose less on a +5% / −3% trade, the same quiet-stock effect as before. Win rates did not rise.

What three cycles show: the first 30 minutes, 60 days of history, and the news all fail to reveal which way these stocks go between 10:00 and 10:30. The reliable pattern is the fade. The next step changes the trade itself: buying after the fade ends, holding longer, and bigger targets, which the 20%-a-week goal needs anyway. That requires full-day price data.

Why

These stocks keep drifting down for the rest of the day. Every entry has a negative average move before costs, so no target, stop or holding time can make a long trade profitable on average. Buying after the fade does not mark the bottom, and holding longer only adds to the loss.

What four cycles show: for stocks that run hard at the open, long trades have no edge in any design or with any input tried. The same downward drift is why the short side works. A long-only path needs a different set of stocks.

Cycle 5 result: a new architecture, A7

Verdict

A7, a Direction-Disentangled Transformer, splits what it learns into a "direction" channel and a "move size" channel, with an adversary that keeps move size out of the direction channel. It was rejected: −$10.41 per trade vs −$9.60 for the base model, better in only 2 of 6 years.

Why, and what it taught

The architecture trained as designed, but its direction score stayed tied to move size. The reason is the target: on a +5% / −3% trade, "did the trade make money" depends on how much a stock moves, so the label itself rewards volatility and no adversary can remove that.

Lesson: to learn direction, the model needs a direction-only target, such as the return divided by the stock's own volatility. That is the next version (A7-v2), to be applied to the new stock sets as their data arrives.

Stage 2: timing models (cycles 6–7)

Cycle 6 result: a change of view, A8

Verdict

A8, the Intraday Timing Transformer, stops asking which stock to buy at 10:00 and asks, every five minutes through the day, is now a good moment to buy this stock for the next 30 minutes? It is the best long model so far: −$1.79 per $1,000 trade at 0.5% cost and +$3.21 at 0.25%, against about −$9 to −$10 for every earlier model. It was still rejected by its plan: not reliably better than gradient boosting on the same task, and not yet break even at 0.5%.

Why it worked, and why not yet

Worked: waiting for the right moment skips the morning fade, and a direction-only target (the 30-minute return divided by the stock's own volatility) stopped the model from simply favouring volatile stocks. The timing signal is real and rises steadily with confidence.

Not yet: the best moments rise about 1–1.7% in 30 minutes, about the same as a 1% round-trip cost. Priced at real fills, about 45% of the first picks were in five-minute bars with no trades, which turned +0.07% on paper into −0.18%.

Next (A8-v2): one change at a time: a liquidity rule for the entry, then a longer hold so the expected move outgrows the cost.

Cycle 7 result: when to buy and when to sell, A9

Verdict

A9, a Multi-Horizon Optimal-Stopping Transformer, predicts how much a stock will rise over five horizons (15 minutes to the close), buys where the best outlook is strongest, and sells when its own re-estimate turns negative. It was rejected: −$9.50 per $1,000 trade at 0.5% cost, worse than A8's −$1.79.

Why it failed

The buy decision ranked moments by expected gain in percent: the prediction multiplied by the stock's volatility and by the time left in the day. Both are largest for volatile stocks early in the morning, so 57% of trades started before 10:00, right in the fade, and lost about $16 each. Gradient boosting with the same rule failed the same way, so the rule, not the network, was the problem.

Lesson: never rank trades on anything multiplied by volatility or time left. Rank on the direction-only score.

What worked

The predictions themselves were good. The 60-minute outlook's top 0.1% of moments rose +1.83% before costs, more than A8's best (+1.67% in 30 minutes). Re-scoring while holding beat a fixed exit by about $3.90 per trade.

Next (A9-v2): keep the model and the re-scoring exit, and buy on the 60-minute direction-only score with A8's rule.
