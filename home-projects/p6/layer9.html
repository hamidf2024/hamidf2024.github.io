At a glance
  • Goal: know a property's real hazard and crime exposure — flood, wildfire, smoke, heat, earthquake, wind, drought, crime, and anything else specific to the address — before an insurance quote, a bad commute, or a surprise finds it first.
  • Summary: 7 scored properties (3 California, 3 Texas, 1 Arizona), each rated across 7–8 hazard categories, crime/public-safety data tied to the correct law-enforcement jurisdiction rather than a generic city-wide number, plus property-specific other-risk flags.
  • Inputs: none to start — pick a market tab, or use "Check another property" at the bottom to queue a new address.
  • Outputs: a tier (Low/Moderate/High/Severe) per hazard category with the sourced evidence behind it, a crime table scoped to the correct jurisdiction, flagged property-specific risks, and a one-paragraph headline synthesis per property.

How this page works

For each address, this page scores seven natural-hazard categories (flood, direct wildfire, wildfire smoke/air quality, extreme heat, earthquake, severe wind/storm, and drought), evaluates crime and public safety against the actual law-enforcement jurisdiction rather than a generic city-wide number, and flags other property-specific risks that don't fit either category — insurance availability, septic condition, structural age, tax anomalies and similar issues that only surface with real research.

Built from property-risk research threads Tony ran and shared, cross-checked and substantially extended this session against independent government and public-safety sources: CAL FIRE / Office of the State Fire Marshal, FEMA and Sacramento County flood resources, the California Geological Survey, Sacramento County's and El Dorado County's own hazard-planning and sheriff pages, USDA NRCS parcel-level soil data for the Texas subjects, the Arizona Geological Survey, City of Tempe and Maricopa County resources for the Arizona subject, CrimeGrade.org's published crime-rate data, and First Street Foundation's property-level climate models as surfaced through Redfin. A "check another property" box at the bottom queues the next one.

Read this before treating any tier here as a final answer: these are desk-research risk indicators, not a substitute for a parcel-specific CAL FIRE Fire Hazard Severity Zone lookup, a FEMA/county flood-zone determination, an actual homeowners-insurance quote, or an on-the-ground inspection. Several tools that would give an exact parcel-level answer (CAL FIRE's FHSZ viewer, FEMA's Map Service Center, CGS's EQ Zapp, each Sheriff's crime map) are interactive map applications this session could not query by address directly — where that's the case, this page says so explicitly rather than guessing a precise zone.
Where the numbers come from (click to expand)
  1. Natural hazards — a property-level model score (First Street Foundation, via Redfin's climate-risk panel) paired with independently-researched regional/official context, since a per-property model can miss a county-level pattern a government source captures (this page found one real gap: 437 Forni Rd's wildfire model score understates El Dorado County's mapped Very High Fire Hazard Severity Zone coverage).
  2. Crime & public safety — the correct law-enforcement jurisdiction is identified first (Sheriff vs. city police), then the best available published crime-rate data for that geography, with explicit notes on how closely the reporting geography matches the actual parcel.
  3. Other risks — bespoke to each property: whatever showed up in research as a real, checkable risk that isn't a natural hazard or crime — insurance availability, septic/systems condition, permit history, tax anomalies, access.
  4. Tiers — 🟢 Low / 🟡 Moderate / 🟠 High / 🔴 Severe, assigned per category from the evidence gathered, not a single blended score — unlike risk, a low flood score and a severe heat score shouldn't be averaged into one misleading number.
