Summary

Research report prepared 25 November 2026 for an individual investor. Data run through Q3 2026 (WGC) and October 2026 (individual central banks). The projections are illustrative scenarios built on the author's judgment. They are not forecasts you can rely on, and this report is not investment advice.

Central banks are buying gold because the 2022 freeze of Russia's reserves showed that foreign-currency assets held abroad can be switched off. Gold held at home has no issuer and no counterparty, and it cannot be frozen. Diversification, crisis insurance, worry about US fiscal policy and, for gold-mining countries, plain domestic economics have kept the buying going since. Purchases have run at roughly twice the pre-2022 pace: more than 1,000 tonnes a year in 2022–2024, 863t in 2025 and 675t in the first nine months of 2026. Q3 2026 alone brought 321t, up 42% year on year, and that happened while the gold price was falling (WGC Q3 2026). The publicly named buyers are now few: China, Poland, the Central Asian gold producers and the Czech Republic. Russia and Turkey have become sellers, and about 63% of the buying is not reported to anyone. My base case puts official buying at about 750t in 2027, 700t in 2028 and 650t in 2029, inside ranges of 350–1,100t. That is a high plateau, not a return to the 2010s average of 473t. Over the next few weeks, though, central banks will not set the gold price. Gold has fallen about 36% from its January 2026 record of $5,405 (LBMA) because the Fed is raising rates into an oil shock. Rising real yields, a strong dollar and forced selling by trend-following funds now dominate the price, as they did before 2022. Over 2027–2029 I expect rate hikes and momentum to matter less, and US fiscal risk, threats to Fed independence, ETF flows and Asian buying of bars and coins to matter more.

Frozen reserves turned gold into sanctions-proof insurance

Sanctions risk is what changed the game. In 2022 the G7 and EU immobilised about $280–330bn of Bank of Russia reserves, roughly $200bn of it at Euroclear in Belgium, and committed to keep them frozen until Russia pays reparations (Brookings). Central bank buying jumped from a 2010–2021 average of 473t a year to more than 1,000t a year from 2022 to 2024 (WGC FY2025). Invesco's 2023 survey of 142 sovereign investors found almost 60% saying the Russia sanctions made gold more attractive. In the same survey, 68% of central banks kept gold at home, up from 50% in 2020 (Reuters via Inquirer). India's RBI shows the custody side most clearly. It repatriated about 104t in FY2025-26 and another 60t between March and September 2026, and 84% of its 880.5t is now held in India (Business Standard). Physical moves each year are smaller than the headlines suggest. Only 9% of central banks increased domestic storage in the past year, and the Bank of England is still the most popular vault at 57% (WGC 2026 survey). A Fed economist made the key point: gold hedges sanctions only if it "cannot be custodied in the country of the sanctioning government" (Kitco/Fed note).

De-dollarization is real but slow, and much of what looks like it is price. The dollar's share of allocated FX reserves was 56.7% in Q2 2026 according to the IMF's COFER data. The renminbi's share was only 2.1% (IMF). By market value, gold reached 27% of global official reserves at end-2025, ahead of US Treasuries (22%) and the euro (15%). The ECB points out, however, that at end-2023 prices gold would only have tied the euro at about 16%, so most of the jump is valuation, not tonnage (ECB). More than two-thirds of central banks that factor geopolitical risk into their decisions have not changed their reserve currencies (same source). Expectations are moving faster than actual holdings. 74% of WGC respondents expect the dollar's share to be lower in five years, up from 42% in 2022, and 83% expect gold's share to be higher. China's holdings of US Treasuries fell to $618bn in July 2026, an 18-year low (Axios). The renminbi is not taking the dollar's place. Gold is what reserve managers buy when they diversify away from it.

Diversification and crisis insurance are the motives central banks themselves give. In the 2026 WGC survey, a record 90% said they hold gold for its performance in crises, 84% as a long-term store of value or inflation hedge and 82% for diversification. "Historical legacy" fell from 62% to 46% (WGC). Central banks increasingly size gold holdings with portfolio rules rather than habit. The Czech National Bank says gold's low correlation with other assets lowers the volatility of its results, and its analysis points to about 100t as the right amount (CNB). Poland raised its target to 30% of reserves in September 2025 (Kitco/Reuters). Rules like these create predictable buying when reserves grow and predictable selling when a price spike pushes gold above its target share.

Inflation and fiscal worries explain why buyers ignored high interest rates. From 2005 to 2021, the 10-year TIPS (inflation-protected Treasury) yield explained 84% of gold's price variation. In 2022–23 that fell to 3%, and from 2024 to 7%. RBC attributes the break to central banks becoming a large buyer that is "relatively price-insensitive", and to worries about debt and the dollar (RBC Wealth Management). This explanation is plausible but not proven, because ETF and Asian buying also surged in the same period. Concern about "reserve-currency economies" appears among the reasons central banks give for planned purchases (Kitco on WGC 2026).

Geopolitics decides who buys, not only whether they buy. In an April 2026 survey, 70% of central banks named geopolitics their top risk, and the ECB finds that "central banks with larger gold purchases tend to be in regions with higher external conflict risk" (ECB). 85% of emerging-market and developing-economy respondents call gold a geopolitical hedge. Only 18% of advanced-economy central banks plan to add gold (Kitco). Poland, which borders Ukraine, is the clearest case.

Domestic purchase programmes are a separate kind of buying. Gold-producing countries buy local mine output with local currency, which builds reserves without spending dollars and helps formalise artisanal mining (WGC). These buyers include Uzbekistan, Kazakhstan, Ghana, Mongolia, the Philippines, Bolivia and Serbia, which buys from the Zijin mine. Their net figures swing both ways, because they also sell to rebalance or raise foreign currency. The programmes can be expensive. The IMF found that Ghana's scheme lost more than $1.7bn in 2025 (1.5% of GDP) (IntelliNews/IMF).

The arguments against gold explain the sellers. It pays no interest, it is volatile, and in a crisis it becomes cash. Turkey sold or swapped roughly 130t in March 2026 to defend the lira after the US–Iran war began (ECB). Russia sells gold from its National Wealth Fund to cover a budget deficit projected at 3.2% of GDP (EUToday). This does not undermine the case for holding gold. It shows gold was usable when these countries needed it.

The data: purchases doubled after 2022, and most of it is unreported

Annual net buying went from 77t in 2010 to the 400–650t range through the 2010s. It fell to 255t in 2020, then roughly doubled to above 1,000t a year from 2022. The value of what central banks buy has risen even faster than the tonnage. At 2026 prices, the 675t bought year-to-date is worth about $99bn, more than in any full year before 2025.

The revisions are large, and they matter for anyone reading the headlines. WGC first estimated Q1 2026 at 244t. It later cut that to 57.7t, the weakest quarter in years, and it came at the January price peak (WGC Q2 2026). Buying recovered to 296t in Q2 and 321t in Q3, against a five-year quarterly average of 227t. The pattern is price-sensitive. Central banks paused at the top and bought heavily as the price fell from its Q1 average of $4,873 to $4,262 in Q3. The same pattern appeared in 2025: buying fell 21% while the price rose about 60%, and the ECB said "price effects… likely curbed official demand" (ECB). WGC states plainly that this does not "imply a firm floor for the gold price" (WGC Q3 2026 Outlook). For the full year, WGC expects 2026 to "at least match" 2025's 863t. My own estimate is about 900–975t, because October brought Poland +17t, China +26t, Uzbekistan +7.5t and Czech Republic +2.5t, and the price fell sharply in November.

Unreported buying is now the majority, which limits what the official data can tell us. Only 120t of Q3 2026's 321t was reported by named countries, about 37% (WGC Q3 2026). Reported data in the IMF's International Financial Statistics covered only 34% of WGC's 2024 estimate, down from 47% in 2023 (WGC FY2024). For 2025 the unreported share was 57%. WGC does not name the hidden buyers. Analysts estimate China's real purchases far above what it reports. Goldman estimated 48t in May 2026 against 10t reported and about 35t in July, roughly double the official figure (Money Metals citing Goldman; Kitco). SocGen put 2025 at about 10x the official 25–27t (Kitco). These are estimates and cannot be verified. Even so, the most important single buyer is probably also the least transparent one.

Total official holdings are about 36,500t (WGC/IMF IFS, Jan 2026 edition). The US, the euro-area core and the IMF hold the most, all from long-standing positions. The active accumulators are lower down the table.

China is the most important and least transparent buyer. Its reported purchases come in steps. Holdings were reported at 1,054t from 2009 until July 2015, when the PBoC disclosed a jump to 1,658t (Wikipedia: Gold reserve). Holdings were then flat at 1,948t from 2019 to late 2022, followed by an 18-month streak that included 225t in 2023. The current streak has run 24 consecutive months to October 2026. October's 26t was the largest monthly addition since October 2023 and came "despite [the] price drop". Gold is only 8.1% of China's reserves, against about 26% worldwide (WGC), so diversification logic leaves a lot of room to keep buying. Chinese commentators give reserve optimisation, renminbi internationalisation, great-power rivalry and US stagflation risk as the reasons (China Daily). China's reported buying is clearly contrarian. It added about 1t a month during the 2025 rally and 15–26t a month as prices fell in late 2026.

Poland has been the largest reported buyer three years running: 90t in 2024, 102t in 2025 and about 140t so far in 2026. Holdings went from 102.9t, where they had sat through 2017 (an IFS figure for January 2016 confirms this), to 690t at end-October 2026. On 5 November Governor Glapiński announced that the 700t target had been reached. Gold was 31% of reserves at end-September, so the 30% share target has also been met. Glapiński added: "I see no reason why we should stop buying gold… our gold reserves should grow in line with our overall reserves" (Kitco/Reuters). Sources disagree on October: an ounce count from NBP data gives 17.1t, while Reuters' estimate from the value of holdings gives about 27t. I use the ounce-based figure. In March 2026 there was talk of selling or revaluing part of the gold to help fund defence (Kitco). That shows fiscal pressure can work against a target.

India's RBI bought heavily from 2018 to 2024, taking holdings from 598.6t to 876t, including about 73t in 2024. It then stopped, with holdings unchanged at 880.5t through September 2026 while it brought gold home (Business Standard). The commonly cited 200t purchase from the IMF in 2009 comes from the project's historical notes and is not verified here.

Russia went from accumulator to seller. Verified holdings rose from 1,208t at end-2014 to 2,113t at end-2018 and 2,298.5t at end-2020 (WGC FY2014; IFS Feb 2021). Since December 2025 it has sold about 62t over 10 consecutive months, reaching 2,264t at end-September 2026, the biggest drawdown since 1998. The sales mirror Finance Ministry sales from the National Wealth Fund to cover a deficit of 7.3 trillion roubles (EUToday). WGC records Russia as the only sizeable seller in Q3, at −19t. One source puts the year-to-date sales at 72t, so the exact figure is uncertain.

Turkey treats gold as a liquidity buffer. It was the largest buyer in 2022 (148t) and bought 75t in 2024. It sold about 159t from March to May 2023, during a temporary limit on bullion imports (WGC FY2023). In 2026 the Iran war pushed Brent near $120 and put pressure on the lira. CBRT holdings, on the central bank's own weekly basis, fell from nearly 830t to 693t in March as it opened about 73t of gold swaps and sold some gold outright. Holdings partly recovered in April once a ceasefire allowed the swaps to be closed (Kitco). Net sales stood at 82t year-to-date by August on WGC's basis (WGC). The CBRT's figures and the IMF's run more than 200t apart. Never mix the two.

The Czech Republic shows how a small central bank buys under explicit rules. Its holdings bottomed at 8t at end-2019 after it sold most of its gold from 1998. It has since rebuilt to 90t by October 2026, buying about 2t a month on an "averaging" basis toward 100t by 2028 (CNB).

Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan are gold producers with the right to buy domestic output. Kazakhstan has the right to buy up to 100% of local mine production. Their buying swings with the price. Kazakhstan sold 47t in 2023, cut 10t in 2024, bought a record 57t in 2025 and about 44t so far in 2026. In October 2026 it said it would trim gold to 75% of reserves, from about 78%, because holdings "are starting to resemble a single-asset portfolio" (Kitco). Uzbekistan reached a record 453.5t, 91% of reserves, in October 2026 after adding about 7–8t a month (Kun.uz).

Singapore's MAS held 127.4t for two decades. It added about 26t in 2021 and reached 230t by end-2023. It then sold 10t in 2024 and 15t in 2025 (WGC FY2024; WGC FY2025), and WGC data put it at 193.8t around May 2026. Singapore is now promoting itself as a place where foreign central banks can store gold (FMT).

The United States holds 8,133.5t and has not bought or sold in decades. The gold is carried on the books at the statutory $42.22/oz, a book value of about $11bn against roughly $1tn at market prices (Fortune). The US matters as the cause of other countries' buying, not as a buyer: its sanctions, its tariffs, its deficits and the location of its custody vaults all push others toward gold. The 2025–26 talk of revaluing the gold and the Fort Knox audit produced no action, and any revaluation would need Congress. Among other holders, Germany, Italy and France are not buying, but the location of their gold has become political. France repatriated all its US-held gold in 2025–early 2026, and the Netherlands moved 86t from New York and Ottawa to London. Berlin rejected calls to bring home the roughly 1,236t it holds at the New York Fed (Kitco). Other buyers include Brazil (43t in Sep–Nov 2025, none reported in 2026), Azerbaijan's sovereign fund SOFAZ (38t in Q1–Q3 2025), Iraq (+74.6t over 2020–25, flat in 2026), Hungary (110t, dormant), Serbia (about 4t a year from domestic mine output) and Ghana, which rebuilt to 24.4t in June 2026 after falling to 18.6t (Kitco/Reuters).

Scenario projections point to a 650–750t plateau through 2029

No major forecaster publishes central bank tonnage beyond 2027. Goldman Sachs, the one that goes furthest, assumes about 60t a month through 2026 and 2027 (about 720t a year), and its latest nowcast is 85t a month (Kitco, 13 Nov 2026). Goldman's own series puts the pre-2022 average at 17t a month, so it is not directly comparable with WGC's figures. WGC gives no figure for 2027. The projections below are therefore my own illustrative scenarios. I built them from base rates (473t a year in 2010–21; about 1,000t a year in 2022–25), stated targets, the 2026 run-rate by country, and the drivers above. They are not investment advice.

How to read the table. Each column describes one consistent world, not the extreme for each country taken separately. That is why Russia's "high" figure is more selling: a world of escalating sanctions and war is also one where Russia sells more gold to fund the war. The "unreported/unattributed" line is the residual between WGC's total and the buying named countries report. I set it at 59–72% of the total, in line with 57–66% in 2022–2026. In weak periods the reported figures can turn negative while WGC's total stays positive, as in March 2026, when named countries were net sellers of about 30t (Kitco). For that reason the unreported share is highest in the low scenario. China's row includes reported PBoC buying only. Its probable hidden purchases are in the unreported line.

Each total equals the sum of the country rows plus the unreported line. The full table, with the key assumption for each row, is in data/projections_2027_2029.csv. For comparison: 2025 was 863t, 2026 is likely about 900–975t, and the 2010–21 average was 473t.

The base case falls from 2026's likely 900t-plus to 750t in 2027 and 650t by 2029 for identifiable reasons. Poland's flagship 700t target and the Czech 100t target are being completed. Kazakhstan has said it will rebalance down. Russia keeps selling. Price sensitivity also cuts both ways: if gold recovers toward bank targets of $4,650–5,000 for 2027, tonnage will slow, as it did in 2025. Against that, China's 8% gold share, the record 45% of central banks that plan to buy, and Goldman's view that emerging-market central banks remain "underweight gold" all keep the plateau well above pre-2022 levels. Even the base case means about 2,100t over three years, worth roughly $300bn at $4,500/oz. The single most sensitive input is China's undisclosed buying. The tail risk sits outside these scenarios: a Turkish-style currency crisis in one large holder could swing a single year by 100t or more, as in 2023 and 2026.

Real yields drive the price now, but fiscal risk will matter more in 2027–29

The gold price is set at the margin. Since 2022, price-insensitive central bank buying has set the trend: WGC's model left 20 of gold's 61 percentage points of gain in 2025 unexplained by macro variables and linked that residual to central bank purchases (WGC Gold Outlook 2026). Leveraged, rate-sensitive Western money sets the swings. In 2026 those swings became violent. The Fed under Chair Warsh hiked to 3.75–4.00% in September. Two governors dissented in October in favour of another hike, and futures price a December 2026 hike plus two more by summer 2027 (FOMC minutes). Ten-year Treasury yields went back above 5% and the dollar reached 19–20-month highs. September CPI was 3.7%, driven by a 34% rise in gasoline after the US–Iran war pushed Brent above $100 (Fortune). WGC's model attributes the −11.8% March crash mainly to momentum, with trend-following CTA funds unwinding after a moving-average break, and the −8.5% September fall to a 53bp jump in yields and a 2% dollar rise (WGC March; WGC September). The war hurt gold rather than helping it. It worked through oil, then inflation, then Fed hikes, which is a warning against treating geopolitics as automatically good for gold.

What will matter more than it does now. US fiscal arithmetic and threats to Fed independence are the factors most likely to move up the ranking. Debt is heading toward 108% of GDP by 2030 on CBO projections that assumed a 4.3% ten-year yield in 2027, a full point below today's. If slower growth or political pressure brings rate cuts while inflation is still above target, gold gets falling real yields and a hedge against debasement together. That combination drove the 2024–25 rally. ETF flows matter more simply because the pool is larger: record holdings can be sold, and a Fed cutting cycle has historically brought money back. Lower prices are reviving Asian physical demand: Chinese bar and coin buying hit a record while jewellery demand collapsed. Equity-crash risk is higher because of AI valuations and widening data-centre credit spreads, although its first effect on gold is usually negative.

What will matter less. The Fed's hiking cycle is close to its peak. Fed staff and the IMF both see disinflation through 2027, and oil has already fallen back from above $100. Rates will still matter, but as a tailwind that is not the only thing in charge. Momentum's damage is largely done, because positioning has been cut. Central bank buying I mark as stable, not rising. My own projections show tonnage easing as targets are met. Its role as a floor under the price remains, and its upside skew is the main reason the bear case is limited. The strongest opposing view comes from WGC itself, which warns that price-sensitive official buying does not guarantee a floor. The 2026 crash shows that a 36% fall is possible even while central banks buy at a record pace for the time of year.

Conclusion

The central bank story is now less about whether official institutions buy gold and more about how they behave. Buyers that target tonnes (Poland, the Czech Republic, probably China) buy dips and step up as prices fall. Buyers that target a share of reserves, and gold-producing countries, sell into rallies. Buyers that use gold as a liquidity buffer (Turkey, Russia, Ghana) sell when under stress. The result is a market with a structural, contrarian official bid that slows a crash without preventing it, and that grows when prices are cheapest. The large and rising share of hidden buying means the headline country data will keep understating the bid. Watching WGC's quarterly totals and its revisions tells you more than the monthly statements from individual central banks.

For an investor, three signals matter most. First, the timing of the Fed's peak and first cut, which sets the near-term price. Second, whether China's reported buying stays at 10t-plus a month as the price recovers; if it does, the plateau is holding. Third, any new reserve freeze, seizure or Fed-independence event, which would push official demand toward the 1,100t high scenario. The base case, with buying easing to 650–750t a year, is consistent with banks' 2027 price targets of $4,650–5,000. Those targets have been cut repeatedly this month, however, and every number in this report should be treated as a range, not a prediction.

Data caveats

  • Unverified historical rows are excluded. Rows labelled HIST or APPROX in the CSV files (for example China 395t in 2000, Russia 788t in 2010, Poland 102.9t through 2017, Brazil ~129.7t in 2021–24) were recalled, not re-checked, and are not charted. The single exception is Poland’s Jan-2016 value of 102.9t, which an IFS table confirms.
  • Estimates and derived values are marked. Hollow markers and hatched bars show values derived by subtraction or estimated (e.g. Russia end-2024, Kazakhstan/Uzbekistan end-2024, Turkey Aug 2026, 2024 Q1/Q3 and 2025 Q1 quarterly figures, the 2026 average price).
  • WGC revises its estimates every quarter, often heavily. Q1 2026 was first estimated at 244t and later cut to 57.7t; 2022 was first published at 1,136t (now 1,082t); 2024 at 1,045t (now 1,092t). The 2010–2019 figures are “as published at the time”: they average about 444t a year versus 473t in WGC’s current series. The 2019 figure (650t) may be ~605t in the current series (unverified).
  • Gold price sources disagree in late November 2026. Quotes in the week to 25 Nov span about $3,470–3,770/oz; the last confirmed LBMA benchmark was $3,870 on 16 Nov. The peak is given variously as $5,405 (LBMA PM, 29 Jan), $5,417, $5,518 and $5,589 (intraday). Check the LBMA benchmark before acting.
  • Turkey’s holdings are not comparable over time. IMF editions switch between bases (with or without commercial-bank and Treasury gold), and CBRT weekly figures run more than 200t above the IMF basis. Never mix the two.
  • Russia stopped reporting to the IMF after January 2022; later figures come from the CBR and are partly estimated. Sources disagree on 2026 sales (62t vs 72t year to date).
  • Reported data cover a shrinking share of buying. IMF IFS covered only 34% of WGC’s 2024 estimate; ~63% of Q3 2026 buying was unattributed. Analyst estimates of China’s hidden buying (Goldman, SocGen) cannot be verified.
  • Some sources conflict. Poland’s October 2026 purchase is 17.1t by NBP ounce count vs ~27t in a Reuters value-based estimate (17.1t used). Singapore’s ~May 2026 WGC figure (193.8t) conflicts with the researcher’s recollection of ~230t.
  • Projections are the author’s illustrative scenarios built from base rates, stated targets and 2026 run-rates. No major forecaster publishes central bank tonnage beyond 2027. Value columns ($bn) are derived (tonnes × average price × 32,150.7 oz/t).
